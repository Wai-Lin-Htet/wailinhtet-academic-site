Business IT & MBA Thesis Guide

This page is for MBA students writing a thesis, especially in Business IT. Work through the 7-step thesis guide — from choosing a topic to the defense — then use the Study Hub to build the knowledge behind each chapter.

1

Choose your topic

Pick a Business IT problem you care about — and one you can actually study.

  • Pick an area: AI in business, digital transformation, IT management, e-commerce, data analytics, or cybersecurity for business.
  • Make it narrow: one organisation, one process, one clear question — not a whole industry.
  • Check the data: can you get the numbers or the people you need? Check before you commit.
  • Talk to your supervisor: discuss the idea early and get feedback before you start writing.

Tip: A good topic is small, clear and measurable — “How AI chatbots change customer service in one bank” beats “AI in business”.

Common mistake: Choosing a topic that is too broad, or one with no data available.

2

Write the research proposal

The proposal is your plan — it tells the reader what you will study and how.

  • Problem statement: what is wrong, who it affects, and why it matters.
  • Objectives: set 3–5 clear objectives for what your study will achieve.
  • Research questions: write one question for each objective.
  • Scope and limitations: say honestly what your study covers — and what it does not.

Tip: Every research question should lead to one objective, and every objective to one part of your findings.

Common mistake: Writing objectives and questions that do not match each other.

3

Literature review

Show what others already know — and where your study adds something new.

  • Search academic sources: journal articles, books and trusted reports — not random web pages.
  • Read with a question in mind: what does this source say, and how does it connect to my topic?
  • Organise by theme: group similar ideas together, not one author after another.
  • Cite everything: give every source a correct citation and keep a full reference list.

Tip: A good review compares and criticises sources — it does not just list them one by one.

Common mistake: Copying long summaries of each paper instead of synthesising what they mean together.

4

Research methodology

Choose how you will answer your research questions — and explain why.

  • Choose the approach: quantitative (numbers, surveys), qualitative (interviews, case study) or mixed methods.
  • Explain your choice: why this approach fits your question — and what its limits are.
  • Design your instrument: survey questions or an interview guide.
  • Describe your sample: who you study, how many, and how you choose them.

Tip: The method follows the question — a “how many” question needs numbers; a “why” question needs interviews.

Common mistake: Choosing a method because it looks easy, not because it answers the question.

5

Data collection & analysis

Collect clean data, analyse it honestly, and present what it actually shows.

  • Collect carefully: keep your data clean — check for missing or wrong entries.
  • Analyse simply first: counts, averages and percentages for surveys; themes for interviews.
  • Present with tables and charts: each one needs a title and a short explanation.
  • Report honestly: include results that surprise you — do not hide them.

Tip: Label every table and chart, and explain in words what the reader should see in it.

Common mistake: Hiding or ignoring data that does not support your expectation.

6

Write the thesis

Put it together in the standard chapter structure — and keep the chapters connected.

  • Follow the standard structure: Ch 1 Introduction, Ch 2 Literature Review, Ch 3 Methodology, Ch 4 Findings & Analysis, Ch 5 Conclusion & Recommendations.
  • Write clearly: formal academic English — short sentences, one idea per paragraph.
  • Connect every chapter: the findings must answer the questions from Chapter 1.
  • Proofread: check grammar, spelling and consistent citations.

Tip: Write a little every day — a finished draft you can improve beats a perfect first page.

Common mistake: Writing chapters that do not connect — findings that answer a different question from the one you asked.

7

Defense / viva preparation

Show the examiners that the work is yours — and that you understand it.

  • Prepare a short presentation: 10–15 minutes telling the story — problem, method, findings, conclusion.
  • Practise likely questions: why this topic, why this method, what your findings mean, what you would do differently.
  • Know your own thesis: examiners will ask about your tables, your sample and your conclusions.
  • Bring your notes: a copy of the thesis and your key points for the day.

Tip: If you do not know an answer, say so honestly and explain how you would find out.

Common mistake: Reading slides word for word instead of speaking to the examiners.

Work through the steps in order — each step builds on the one before it. If you are stuck on a step, ask your teacher before moving on.
